Start from what the course and the judges ask for.

The ASME Innovative Additive Manufacturing 3D (IAM3D) Challenge was held as part of ASME EFx at Northeast Wisconsin Technical College in Green Bay, Wisconsin, and concluded on May 4, 2024. The 2023–2024 challenge was the Unmanned Aerial Racing Cargo Vehicle (U.A.R.C.V.) challenge. Teams designed, manufactured, integrated and tested an FPV-piloted drone that had to fly an indoor obstacle course while picking up and dropping off payloads. Entries were judged on a technical design report, effective use of additive manufacturing and timed course performance. SDSU’s team placed fourth internationally.

That brief gives the aircraft three jobs: get around the course quickly, hold a payload through every maneuver, and release it without anyone touching it. Because additive manufacturing was graded, the airframe also had to show why its geometry was printed that way, not just that it was printed.

Turn the brief into systems with clear boundaries.

The design report describes the work in four phases: research, design, manufacturing and testing. Research ended in three design problems that the flight hardware could not solve on its own: a payload system that picks up, carries and releases a cube; an alignment system that lets the pilot line up with the payload; and a battery system that slots in and out easily while holding the pack in flight.

Each of those problems became a printed mechanism that hangs off a common airframe, so the frame, the mechanisms and the electronics were designed against one another instead of one after the other.

One electrical limit sets three design inputs. The ESC’s 55 A rating caps each motor at 40% throttle. MEPS thrust data puts that point at 0.517 kg, or 5.072 N, per motor, and four motors together match the report’s 20.29 N maximum upward force. That number does three jobs. It is the load case for the frame analysis, it sets the conservative acceleration used to size the payload magnet, and it bounds the power the battery must deliver in bursts.

Mass runs through every row. The battery was chosen on weight and discharge rate before capacity, the ESC and flight controller as a single compact stack, and the camera mechanism on the smallest servo that worked. The bill of materials, including filament, comes to $496.56.

Choose a mechanism whose safe state matches the mission.

The team compared electrical and mechanical ways to hold a payload. An energize-to-hold electromagnet would draw current and heat up for the whole lap, and the flight controller cannot drive one without external circuitry. An energize-to-release layout avoids the continuous draw, but both electromagnet paths depended on whether custom circuits were allowed. The rulebook did not say, and follow-up rule inquiries did not settle it, so the team dropped them.

What it chose was a permanent magnet for retention and a mechanical release. If the servo loses power in flight, the payload stays held. Retention costs no current and no heat, and the servo works only at the moment of release.

The report sizes the magnet from the payload up. No payload weight was given, so the team estimated it: a 1 in printed cube at 11.04 g plus a washer at 11.19 g gives 22.23 g. The magnet has to hold that mass during acceleration, so the force requirement is m(g + a). Taking the 20.29 N maximum thrust against the 0.37 kg minimum mass of the required components gives an acceleration ceiling of 54.837 m/s², higher than the finished aircraft can reach. That yields a minimum retention force of 1.4371 N. The selected magnet is rated at 5.829 N, about four times the requirement, and a pull-force check against the washer thickness confirmed the rating applies to this payload interface.

Turn half a servo rotation into a predictable separation.

Release means pulling the payload far enough from the magnet that the attraction falls below the payload’s weight. Magnet-force data shows that takes a gap of at least 0.1875 in. Micro servos rotate only about 180 degrees, so a lead screw has to deliver that travel in half a revolution. That fixes a custom pitch of 0.375 in per turn, which is easy to make because the screw is printed.

A lead-screw force model then gives the drive torque. It loads the screw with the magnet’s full 1.31 lb pull and uses a friction coefficient of 0.42, the worst case for PLA, and returns 0.02 N·m. The smallest servo, 2.1 g, stalls just under that, so the team stepped up to a 4.3 g servo rated at 0.037 N·m, about 1.85 times the requirement for 2.2 g more mass.

In operation the magnet stays fixed. The servo turns the lead screw, a nut drives a platen against the payload, and the payload is pushed clear of the magnet.

Widen the pilot’s view without a second video system.

A second camera would give dedicated downward vision, but the goggles on the market take one video source, most flight controllers cannot stream two outputs or switch quickly, and a two-monitor ground station is expensive and hard to run. The team designed a mechanical mount that turns the single DJI camera instead.

Three layouts were built and compared. A geared mount rotated the camera but used a lot of volume, needed at least 0.5 in of wire clearance behind the camera, and had to be assembled in two halves. A top-mounted servo removed the gears, but it put the camera lower than normal and would have changed how the pilot judges the aircraft’s size. The selected layout puts the servo underneath and swings the camera on a pivot arm around the frame, so the normal forward position stays above the frame and less material is used.

The design target was a 0.25 s camera move. Torque for the arm’s inertia is 0.000224 N·m and for the arm’s weight 0.0000656 N·m. The 8 g camera at a 20 mm lever adds 0.00157 N·m, for a total of about 0.00186 N·m. The 2.1 g servo’s stall torque is just under 0.02 N·m, roughly ten times that need, so the lightest servo was enough here while the payload needed the larger one.

Packaging shaped the frame too. The topology studies gave the front extra material to carry the camera mount and add rigidity, and the camera mount’s print orientation balances support material against load direction.

Use geometry to resist the direction of motion.

The battery system compared two straps against one strap with grip tape on weight, grip and overall performance. The lighter single strap won, but friction alone is not asked to hold the pack against fore–aft movement. Grooves locate the strap, and raised end walls form a positive geometric stop against acceleration.

Remove material only after defining the load case.

The analysis set out to answer one question: how light can the frame be before the arms flex too much under motor thrust? The load case came from the ESC limit. At 40% throttle each motor pushes with 5.072 N. A baseline SolidWorks study of the original frame, with all intended holes and component masses distributed, gave about 0.34 mm of maximum arm displacement, and the team set 0.5 mm as the limit any lighter candidate had to stay under.

Altair Inspire topology studies then ran at four mass-fraction targets, with the front and back given extra material for the camera mount and rigidity, and a split so the rear holes stayed connected to the frame. Each candidate was checked with a static analysis of accelerating to maximum throttle from rest, with mass included through inertia relief.

All four stayed under 0.5 mm, with the 20% design only just inside it. The optimized shapes were difficult to export in a printable form, so the four designs were used as guidance for a frame rebuilt by hand in SolidWorks. The refined design projects a 27% mass reduction while staying under the displacement limit. That is less inert mass for the same propulsion, with stiffness still bounded.

Check the operating band, not just a static snapshot.

Static stiffness cannot show whether the motors excite a structural mode while the aircraft is flying. The team extracted thirty modes and ran a harmonic-response study with the lowest allowable damping, so that results would err high. A hand calculation using cantilever-beam stiffness checked the simulation setup: it gave about 0.00054 mm against roughly 0.00055 mm from SolidWorks at maximum planned throttle.

The study focused on 1152–1779.1 Hz, the band associated with sustained operating throttle. Displacements across it were negligible except near 1220 Hz, where the response is significant but still below the 0.5 mm limit. The report notes that its thrust-versus-frequency input comes from prolonged-throttle bench data, so real amplitudes at lower frequencies should be smaller than plotted. A final Inspire check combining peak force from sudden acceleration with the vibration response also stayed under 0.5 mm.

That is a prediction, and the report treats it as one. The analysis narrowed the structural risk before printing, but flying the aircraft is what confirms it.

Design the printed part around layers, clearances and supports.

Material came from its own matrix of cost, strength-to-weight, manufacturability and stiffness. CarbonX carbon-fiber-reinforced PLA tied Formlabs Rigid 10K resin at 3.8. The resin’s small weight saving did not outweigh its cost and manufacturability, while CF-PLA prints in-house on a Bambu Lab X1 Carbon at $0.0064 per gram. In-house SLS and MBJ printing would have raised cost and lengthened the design and test cycle.

FDM layers are weakest in the direction of layer adhesion, so print orientation was a design decision. The frame stays planar so each plate prints flat with no supports: better bed contact, layers aligned with the main in-plane loads, and less warping. The magnet cage prints on its side, with supports, because it takes shear from the payload during lateral acceleration.

Fit needed its own validation. A hole-clearance test on this printer found that 0.15 mm (0.006 in) was the free-moving fit, so every screw hole was drawn 0.006 in larger than its screw to avoid undersized holes cracking from layer expansion. Components were printed more than once to confirm reliability, and spares were printed in case a part broke before or during competition.

What this project taught about building flying hardware.

Design for repairability.

The frame is two flat printed plates on standoffs, the mechanisms bolt on with M3 and M2 hardware, and the report records that spares were printed. A broken guard costs one part, not a rebuilt assembly.

Test under realistic conditions.

Flight testing was still pending when the report was written. Simulation bounded frame deflection and resonance, but only flying the course checks balance, pilot view and the payload under real maneuvering. Closing that gap earlier is the clearest improvement for a next iteration.

Manage the interfaces.

The payload cage, camera pivot and battery plate all depend on the same frame. The optimization needed extra material at the front for the camera mount, the cage orientation was chosen for shear, and every screw hole used the measured 0.15 mm clearance. Change one and the others move.

Balance performance with schedule and resources.

The team rebuilt optimizer output by hand instead of chasing a perfect topology, printed in-house instead of using SLS or MBJ, and came in at a $496.56 bill of materials. Each choice traded some performance for time and cost.

Keep the aircraft serviceable when things fail.

A detached guard on the pavement and repair work in a hotel room were part of this project. Spares, measured clearances and fastened joints are what made that work practical.
