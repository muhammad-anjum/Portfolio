Keep the car. Replace the operating system.

The Triumph Stag is a four-seat British sports tourer styled by Giovanni Michelotti and built from 1970 to 1977. It left the factory with a 3.0-liter V8, rear-wheel drive and a distinctive T-bar structure. This project keeps that character and replaces the propulsion with a modern electric system.

A conversion is more than a motor swap. Battery placement changes mass distribution and service access. The high-voltage path has to coexist with low-voltage controls. Cooling has to serve both the battery and the drive unit. The driver still needs legible feedback.

Define the interfaces before routing the first wire.

The propulsion foundation is an EV West universal conversion package built around a Tesla Model S large drive unit (LDU) and an EV Controls T2-C controller. The LDU combines the motor, inverter and reduction gearbox. The package adds the throttle and brake interfaces, precharge hardware, axles, charge port, charger, DC-DC conversion, contactor hardware and control electronics.

The battery is a fourteen-module Tesla Model S pack. At the listed module ratings that is about 74.2 kWh and 319.2 V nominal, rising to 352.8 V at the modules’ charge cutoff. Both voltages sit inside the drive unit kit’s published 275–400 V input range. The BMS, charger, contactors, cooling and instrumentation still had to be integrated around the car’s actual packaging.

Turn fourteen modules into one known pack.

Each EV West module is rated at 22.8 V nominal and 5.3 kWh, with 444 cylindrical cells arranged 74-parallel by 6-series. Fourteen modules put 6,216 cells in the car. Before integration I checked the modules as electrical assemblies: confirming cell-group readings, inspecting connections and coolant interfaces, and establishing that the set could run together under one BMS.

Assembly then became a packaging and repeatability problem. Modules had to be retained without loading vulnerable features, joined with controlled high-voltage connections, wired to the cell-monitoring harnesses, and left accessible enough to diagnose. The enclosure work splits the pack across locations in the car while keeping a single electrical architecture.

Make every measurement believable before trusting the pack.

Programming the BMS started with configuration, but troubleshooting depended on evidence. Module count and cell topology had to match the physical harness. Cell-group voltages had to be plausible and consistent. Temperature inputs, balancing, pack limits and communications had to agree with what the hardware was doing.

I used live serial data and in-vehicle checks to trace mismatches between configuration, wiring and reported values. The aim was not just to clear a fault but to decide whether it was an unsafe electrical condition, a wrong parameter, a connection problem or a communications issue, before enabling the next stage.

Control when energy can move, and where it can’t.

The distribution enclosure holds the contactors, precharge path, service disconnect, protection and measurement interfaces between the battery and the inverter. Wiring it took more than continuity. Conductor routing, terminations, strain relief, polarity, insulation and service access all decide whether the system can be energized and diagnosed safely.

Low-voltage command wiring stays legible next to the orange high-voltage conductors, so the contactor sequence and interlocks can be traced during troubleshooting. The finished enclosure is the controlled gateway between stored energy and the rest of the car.

Treat fluid routing as part of the electrical system.

The Tesla modules have integrated liquid-cooling passages, and the drive unit and inverter also depend on managed temperatures. The coolant network has to reach separated hardware without blocking service access or putting fluid near electrical connections.

My work covered connecting the module interfaces, routing lines through the available structure, coordinating pumps and auxiliary power, and checking the physical system before sustained operation. Hose support, bend radius, high points that trap air, bleed access and distance from terminals were installation constraints from the start.

Translate driver inputs into controlled torque.

The T2-C sits between the Stag’s driver controls and the Tesla inverter. Tuning meant configuring the controller around the installed hardware, then checking throttle and brake inputs, direction and drive-state logic, torque response, regeneration, current limits and reported temperatures, without jumping to full output.

In parallel I designed and built the custom low-voltage harness. It distributes power and signals to the BMS, controller, dash, charger interfaces, pumps, fans, relays, switches and retained vehicle features. I traced wiring and communications faults as each subsystem came online.

Make charging a coordinated vehicle state.

The charger had to match the fourteen-module pack while staying subordinate to the BMS. Configuration tied the charger’s voltage and current behavior to pack limits, cell monitoring, contactor control and fault handling. The charge inlet, high-voltage path, DC-DC system and low-voltage wake-up had to work as one sequence.

Bring-up was staged: verify communication and sensed values, confirm pack state, set the permitted charging conditions, then watch cell-group and temperature data as energy went in.

System principle

The charger doesn’t decide that the pack is healthy. It works inside limits the battery-management system sets and monitors.

Expose the data needed to drive, and the data needed to debug.

The digital dash evolved alongside the car. Early layouts proved the communication path and basic gauge behavior. Later versions reorganized the screen around driver priority: pack voltage, drive mode, motor speed, current, power, throttle and brake state, and temperatures across the inverter and motor-control hardware.

I programmed and calibrated the dash for real-time telemetry, and used each stage to judge legibility, hierarchy and whether the displayed signals matched the controller and BMS. Five stages show the progression.

The first screen only has to prove two things: that the display works, and that the data path from the car reaches it. Nothing about layout is decided yet.

Prove the interfaces in sequence, then prove the vehicle.

Commissioning joins work that was verified one subsystem at a time. The sequence moves from low-voltage power and communications, through BMS plausibility and interlocks, contactors and precharge, charger and cooling auxiliaries, inverter readiness and driver inputs, to motor response and complete telemetry.
